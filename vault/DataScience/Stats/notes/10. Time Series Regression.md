### 1. [[Linear Regression|Simple]] vs. [[Multiple Linear Regression|Multiple Linear Regression]] (5 & 15-Mark Questions)

Regression calculates the "Line of Best Fit" through your data points using the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method.

- **Simple Linear Regression:** Predicts a target variable ($Y$) using exactly **one** predictor variable ($X$). In time series, $X$ is often simply "Time" ($t$).
    
    - _Formula:_ $Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_t + \epsilon_t$
        
- **Multiple Linear Regression:** Predicts a target variable using **two or more** predictor variables.
    
    - _Formula:_ $Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1t} + \beta_2 X_{2t} + \dots + \epsilon_t$
        
    - _Example:_ Predicting daily ice cream sales ($Y$) using Temperature ($X_1$), Weekend Indicator ($X_2$), and Price ($X_3$).
        

### 2. Lagged Variables (5-Mark Question)

- **Definition:** A lagged variable is a past value of a variable used as a predictor in a regression model. Instead of looking at $X_t$ (today's value), you use $X_{t-1}$ (yesterday's value) or $X_{t-k}$.
    
- **The Role in Forecasting:** Real-world events take time to have an effect. This is called the "Delay Effect".
    
    - _Example:_ If a company runs a massive TV advertisement today ($t$), sales won't spike instantly. The sales spike might happen next week. Therefore, to predict next week's sales, you must use a **Lagged Variable** of Advertising Spend ($X_{t-7}$).
        

### 3. Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) (5-Mark Question)

- **Definition:** $R^2$ is a statistical metric that measures the **Goodness of Fit** of a regression model. It represents the percentage of total variance in the target variable ($Y$) that is successfully explained by your predictor variables ($X$).
    
- **Interpretation:** It ranges from 0 to 1 (or 0% to 100%).
    
    - An $R^2$ of 0.85 means: _"85% of the fluctuations in our sales can be explained by our regression model. The remaining 15% is random noise or missing variables."_
        
    - The higher the $R^2$, the tighter your data points hug the regression line.
        

### 4. Residual Analysis and Diagnostics (15-Mark Question)

Once your computer calculates the regression line, you are left with **Residuals** (the Errors).

$$e_t = \text{Actual } (Y_t) - \text{Predicted } (\hat{Y}_t)$$

To get full marks on the 15-mark model diagnostics question, you must state that a valid regression model must pass the **Three Assumptions of Residuals**:

1. **Zero Mean:** The residuals must hover evenly around the 0 line.
    
2. **Homoscedasticity (Constant Variance):** The spread of the errors must remain constant over time. If the errors start small and get wider and wider (like a megaphone shape), the model fails.
    
3. **No Autocorrelation (Independence):** This is the most critical rule in time series! Yesterday's error should not help you predict today's error. The residuals must be pure, random **White Noise**.
    
    - _How to test:_ We use the **Durbin-Watson Test** or plot the ACF chart of the residuals. If the ACF shows significant spikes, autocorrelation exists, and the model is invalid!
