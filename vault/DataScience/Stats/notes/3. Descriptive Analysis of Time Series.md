## 1. Measures of Central Tendency and Dispersion 
### central tendency [[DataScience/Stats/notes/2. Descriptive stats vs  inferential statistics]]
- here we  mostly use The Rolling mean as its the most versatile measure

 
In a standard dataset, the mean $(\mu)$ is just the sum of all values divided by the number of values.

But in time series, because we need to see how the center changes over time, 
we use a Simple Moving Average (SMA).

Here is the exact formula for a rolling mean at time $t$ with a window size of $k$:
$$SMA_t = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} X_{t-i}$$

here:
$SMA_t$ (Simple Moving Average at time $t$): This is your final answer. It is the new, smoothed-out data point you are calculating for "today" (or whatever specific time $t$ represents).

$k$ (Window Size): The total number of data points you are averaging together. If you are calculating a 5-day moving average, $k=5$.

$\frac{1}{k}$ (The Division): Multiplying by $\frac{1}{k}$ is the exact same math as adding everything up and dividing by $k$. It is the standard mathematical way to write "take the average."

$\sum$ (Sigma / Summation): The giant E-shaped symbol is the universal math command for "add everything up."

$i=0$ to $k-1$ (The Loop): These numbers attached to the top and bottom of the Sigma tell you exactly how many times to run the addition loop.

$i=0$ is where you start counting.

$k-1$ is where you stop.  
(If $k=5$, you stop when $i=4$. So you loop for $i=0,1,2,3,4$, which is exactly 5 times.)

$X_{t-i}$ (The Raw Data Point): This is the actual number from your dataset at a specific time.

When $i=0$, the math asks for $X_{t-0}$ (Today's data).

When $i=1$, the math asks for $X_{t-1}$ (Yesterday's data).

When $i=2$, the math asks for $X_{t-2}$ (The day before yesterday's data).

Putting it all together into a plain-English sentence:

"To find the smoothed value for today $(SMA_t)$, start with today's raw data $(X_t)$ and add up the raw data from the previous days $(X_{t-i})$ until you have added a total of $k$ days. Finally, divide that giant sum by $k$."

in sumarry:

If $k=5$ (a 5-day window), 
the math looks at today $(X_t)$ 
and the previous four days $(X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, \dots)$, 
adds them up, and divides by $5$.

Tomorrow, it drops the oldest day and adds the newest day.

This creates a dynamic, moving baseline that filters out daily noise.

## 3. Seasonality and trend analysis

goal is to figure out exactly how the seasonal spikes and the long-term trend are interacting with each other or
When the baseline trend changes, does the seasonal spike also change?

we find it using the Additive and Multiplicative models 

Additive Model:
$$Y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t$$
*(Raw Data = Trend + Seasonality + Residuals)*
- When the trend is at 5,000 players, the weekend spike is +500.
- no matter what happens the +500 will be there every weekends  
- trend has no effect on the season

Multiplicative Model:
$$Y_t=T_t*S_t*R_t$$
*(Raw Data = Trend * Seasonality * Residuals)*
- When the trend is at 5,000 players, the weekend spike is 10%.
- according to trend the weekend spike is effected 
- trend has effect on the season
